106,987appointments
60,270unique patients
20.3%baseline no-show rate
0%missing values

Queried entirely in SQL using DuckDB directly against a pandas DataFrame — CTEs, window functions, conditional aggregation, and date arithmetic — on the public healthcare system of Vitória, Brazil.

Waiting time is the strongest driver found: no-show risk jumps from 4.7% on same-day bookings to 34.4% once the wait passes a month, then plateaus.
SMS reminders looked like they backfired — until controlled for wait time: naively, SMS recipients no-show more (27.7% vs 16.7%), but within every wait-time bucket, SMS recipients actually no-show less. The naive result was confounded.
Younger patients no-show more (23.8% for 18–35 vs 15.5% for 66+), while patients managing a chronic condition attend more reliably — routine monitoring likely helps.
Scholarship (welfare) recipients no-show more (23.8% vs 19.9%) — a likely cost or transport barrier worth investigating directly.
1,007 repeat patients (4.3%) have missed every appointment they've ever booked — a 100% personal no-show rate, and a clear flag for phone confirmation instead of automated reminders.

1,941listings, 2 datasets merged
R² 0.85best model (Gradient Boosting)
+123%price lift for "mewah" listings
p<0.001Jaksel vs Tebet price gap

Merged two Kaggle listing datasets with different schemas, removed 70 duplicate rows, and log-transformed price to fix severe right-skew (6.00 → 0.12) before modeling.

Unique angle — mining the listing titles: 14 keyword + sub-location features extracted via regex from 1,010 Tebet listing titles. "Mewah" (luxury) listings sell for 123% more on average; "minimalis" and "baru" correlate with lower prices.
Jaksel is 31% pricier per m² than the Tebet market average (Mann-Whitney U test, p = 2.19×10⁻²³).
K-Means found 3 natural market segments: Economical–Mid (1,414 homes, Rp6.72M), Premium (521 homes, Rp27.66M), and a small Multi-Unit/Kos outlier cluster (6 homes, ~25 bedrooms).
Gradient Boosting was the best predictor (R² 0.85, MAE Rp4.0M) — beating Random Forest (0.84) and Linear Regression (0.61).
Ablation study proved the text-mining features earned their place: adding them lifted R² by +0.5% and cut MAE from Rp1.86M to Rp1.82M — a measurable, not decorative, contribution.

1,076transactions, Aug 2022–Nov 2023
94→9product names normalized to categories
9forecasting models compared
19.2%best model MAPE

A full audit before analysis surfaced four data-quality issues: 94 inconsistently-typed product name variants ("Dupleks310" / "Duplex310" / "dupleks310"), 40 duplicate rows (kept — legitimate in daily B2B orders), and 39% of calendar days with zero transactions, which is why forecasting was done weekly rather than daily.

Product concentration is steep: the top 3 categories generate 77.4% of revenue; a classic Pareto pattern where 13 of 87 products (15%) drive 80% of revenue.
Simple beat complex: Simple Exponential Smoothing was the best single-split model at 19.2% MAPE — while SARIMA, a "proper" time-series model, underperformed even the naive baseline (38.7% vs 35.3%).
A single evaluation can mislead in either direction: rolling-origin backtesting across 6 time points showed SARIMA actually averaging 35.5% MAPE (± 18.6) — better than its single-split result, not worse. One split isn't enough to trust.
ABC-XYZ classification flagged 2 high-value, stable-demand products for automatic restocking, and 53 low-value, volatile products as make-to-order candidates.
Delivered as four artifacts: a 107-cell analysis notebook, a 4-tab interactive Streamlit dashboard, an 11-sheet Excel export, and a documented GitHub repository.

89,741songs, cleaned from 114K
k = 3optimal clusters (silhouette 0.38)
72.9%low-energy cluster → Depression
21.4%pseudo R² (McFadden)

My undergraduate thesis. Genre-based music organization isn't emotionally specific, and Spotify's perceptual features are black-box and access-limited — so this study tests whether two purely physical, self-computable features (Loudness in dB, Tempo in BPM) can predict emotion on their own.

Three natural clusters emerged: High Intensity (−6.6dB, 151 BPM), Low Intensity (−19.4dB, 104 BPM), and Moderate (−7.3dB, 104 BPM), confirmed by both the Elbow method and silhouette score.
A strong, linear match on the low-energy end: the Low Intensity cluster maps to Depression 72.9% of the time — quiet, slow songs reliably read as sad (χ² = 23,458.82, p < 0.001).
A partial match on the high-energy end: High and Moderate clusters split between Exuberance and Anxious — Loudness and Tempo predict Arousal well, but can't distinguish positive from negative emotion (Valence) on their own.
Loudness dominates as a predictor: in the multinomial logistic regression, Loudness's Wald statistic (W² = 15,340) dwarfs Tempo's (W² = 682) — both significant, but Loudness carries far more weight.

The smaller model won: a 32/64-filter CNN reached 94.4% validation accuracy with fewer false positives and false negatives than a larger 64/128-filter version (93.0%).
More capacity didn't help at this input resolution (48×48px) — the larger model was likely more prone to overfitting rather than better generalization.
